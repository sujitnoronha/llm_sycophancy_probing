1. Research Thesis
1.1 The One-Sentence Claim
Core Thesis
RLHF-trained language models have learned implicit authority hierarchies from human preference data: when a user self-identifies as high-status (professor, doctor, CEO), models become measurably more sycophantic — and this authority-deference behavior is encoded as a linear direction in the model’s residual stream that causally overlaps with the sycophancy direction, creating a mechanistic pathway from persona detection to truthfulness failure.

1.2 Why This Matters
The sycophancy literature has treated “the user” as a monolith. Sharma et al. (ICLR 2024), Perez et al. (2022), and Wei et al. (2023) all measure sycophancy against a generic, unidentified user. But in real deployments, users arrive with identities, credentials, and social signals. The question nobody has asked is: sycophantic toward whom?
There is strong theoretical reason to expect persona effects. RLHF training data comes from human annotators who themselves carry authority biases. When evaluating two model responses — one that agrees with a “professor” and one that pushes back — annotators are more likely to rate agreement as “preferred.” The model learns a conditional distribution: P(agree | high-status user) > P(agree | low-status user). This is a form of reward hacking from biased preference data that has never been measured.
The safety implications are immediate. If a voice AI medical assistant is more likely to agree with a patient who says “I’m a doctor” than one who says “I don’t know much about medicine,” it provides systematically worse truthfulness to people who claim authority. Anyone can claim to be a professor. Authority-biased sycophancy is trivially exploitable social engineering.
1.3 What Makes This a COLM Paper (Not a Workshop Paper)
The behavioral finding alone — “models are more sycophantic to professors” — is predictable and therefore insufficient. What elevates this to COLM is the mechanistic analysis via probing classifiers across five architectures. Specifically:
	•	A universal structural finding: If the authority–sycophancy direction overlap is high across all five models from four different labs, that reveals something fundamental about how RLHF creates authority deference — not just a quirk of one model.
	•	The authority vs. confidence disentanglement: By probing for both authority (persona identity) and confidence (linguistic assertiveness) and comparing their overlap with sycophancy, we definitively answer whether models respond to who you claim to be or how confidently you speak. Either answer reshapes how we think about sycophancy mitigation.
	•	The domain-match test: If a physics professor gets deferred to on history questions (where their expertise is irrelevant), that proves blind authority deference — not rational expertise weighting. This specific finding has never been tested and is the key novelty that prevents the “obvious result” reviewer objection.
	•	Principled remediation: Subtracting the probed authority direction to reduce persona-biased sycophancy is a mechanistically-grounded intervention, not a prompt-engineering hack.

Axis 2: Domain Match (2 levels)
	•	Matched: The persona’s claimed expertise aligns with the question domain. A physics professor pushes back on a physics question.
	•	Mismatched: The persona’s claimed expertise is irrelevant. A physics professor pushes back on a history question.

This is the critical novelty axis. If matched > mismatched authority sycophancy, the model is doing something semi-rational (weighting relevant expertise). If matched ≈ mismatched, the model is blindly deferring to status signals regardless of relevance. The latter is the alarming finding.
Axis 3: Confidence Language (2 levels)
	•	High confidence: “I’m absolutely certain the answer is Y. I’ve verified this many times.”
	•	Low confidence: “I might be wrong, but I think the answer could be Y?”

Crossing authority with confidence disentangles two competing hypotheses: (a) models defer to who you are, vs. (b) models defer to how confidently you speak. A high-authority / low-confidence condition (“I’m a professor but I’m not sure...”) vs. a low-authority / high-confidence condition (“I have no background but I’m 100% certain...”) directly pits these against each other.
Factorial Summary
Total conditions: 3 authority levels × 2 domain matches × 2 confidence levels = 12 persona conditions + 4 controls (no-persona with high/low confidence, plus 2 length-matched neutrals) = 16 conditions.
With 50 questions across 5 models: 16 × 50 × 5 = 4,000 conversations total. Each conversation is 4 turns, ~400 tokens. Manageable in a single day of inference.
2.3 Question Bank Design

3. Probing Methodology: The Mechanistic Core
3.1 Why Probing (Not Patching or Logit Lens)
We deliberately chose linear probing as the single mechanistic method, applied rigorously across all five models, rather than using multiple methods on fewer models. The rationale:
	•	Scalability: Probing requires only forward passes to collect activations, then lightweight sklearn logistic regression. This scales to 5 models easily, whereas activation patching (one forward pass per layer per prompt) would consume the entire compute budget on 2 models.
	•	Comparability: Probing produces a single number per model per layer (accuracy, cosine similarity) that is directly comparable across architectures. Patching heatmaps are architecture-specific and hard to aggregate.
	•	Directional analysis: Probing gives us the learned direction vectors (classifier weights), enabling the cosine similarity analysis between authority and sycophancy representations. Patching does not produce directions.
	•	Natural steering: The probed directions can be immediately used as steering vectors for the remediation experiments, creating a coherent narrative from analysis to intervention.
	•	Robustness: Linear probes are well-understood, reproducible, and have fewer hyperparameters than patching setups. Less implementation risk in a 10-day timeline.
3.2 Activation Collection Protocol
For each model and each test conversation, we collect residual stream activations at a specific, carefully chosen token position.
Token Position Selection
Position: The last token of the user’s Turn 3 message (the pushback turn). At this point, the model has processed the persona prefix (Turn 1), its own correct answer (Turn 2), and the full persona-reinforced pushback (Turn 3). The hidden state here encodes the model’s “decision state” — everything it needs to generate Turn 4.
We deliberately do not use the first token of Turn 4 (the model’s response) because that would capture the model’s actual sycophantic behavior rather than the pre-decision representation. We want to probe what the model “knows” before it starts generating, not what it’s already decided.
Layer Selection
We collect activations at every layer for all five models. This is feasible because each activation is a single vector (dimension = hidden size) per prompt per layer. Storage: 200 prompts × 42 layers (max, Gemma) × 4096 dimensions × 4 bytes ≈ 130MB per model. Total across 5 models: under 1GB.
Prompt Subsetting for Probing
We use a subset of the full 4,000 conversations for probing to ensure clean, balanced training data:
	•	200 prompts per model: 50 questions × 4 key conditions (high-authority/high-confidence, high-authority/low-confidence, low-authority/high-confidence, no-persona baseline)
	•	80/20 train/test split stratified by condition and question domain
	•	The remaining 2,000+ conversations per model serve as held-out behavioral data for the steering evaluation
3.3 Three Probes, One Question Each
Probe A: Authority Detection
Question: At which layer does the model represent “this user claims high authority”?
Labels: High-authority persona = 1; Low-authority/no-persona = 0
Expected result: Accuracy near chance (~50%) at early layers, rising to 85–95% at some layer L_auth. This layer is where the model has encoded the persona identity.
What it tells us: The layer at which authority becomes linearly readable reveals how early in processing the model recognizes social status claims. If this happens very early (layers 2–5), the model is treating authority as a surface-level lexical feature. If late (layers 20+), it’s a higher-order semantic interpretation.
Probe B: Sycophancy Prediction
Question: At which layer is the model’s upcoming sycophantic behavior already “decided”?
Labels: Model flipped its answer in Turn 4 = 1; Model held firm = 0
Expected result: Accuracy near chance early, rising to 70–85% at some layer L_syc. Below the authority probe accuracy because sycophancy is a noisier, more complex behavior.
What it tells us: The gap between L_auth (when authority is recognized) and L_syc (when sycophancy is decided) reveals the processing pipeline. If L_auth << L_syc, there’s a multi-layer computation converting authority detection into sycophantic behavior. If L_auth ≈ L_syc, the conversion is near-instantaneous.
Probe C: Confidence Detection (Control Probe)
Question: At which layer does the model represent “this user sounds highly confident”?
Labels: High-confidence language = 1; Low-confidence language = 0
Why this is essential: Without this probe, we cannot disentangle authority from confidence. Professors tend to speak confidently; if the authority probe is really detecting confidence rather than persona identity, the entire finding is confounded. Probe C provides the control: if the authority and confidence directions are orthogonal (low cosine similarity), they are genuinely different features.
3.4 The Core Analysis: Direction Overlap
This is the paper’s single most important analysis. For each model, at the layers where probes are most accurate, we extract the weight vectors and compute pairwise cosine similarities.

The Punchline Analysis
At the peak probing layer for each model, extract the learned weight vectors w_auth, w_syc, w_conf from Probes A, B, C. Compute:

•  cos(w_auth, w_syc) = overlap between authority detection and sycophancy decision
•  cos(w_conf, w_syc) = overlap between confidence detection and sycophancy decision
•  cos(w_auth, w_conf) = overlap between authority and confidence (are they the same feature?)

This produces a 3×3 cosine similarity matrix per model. The paper lives in the off-diagonal numbers.

3.5 Additional Probing Analyses
Layer Trajectory Analysis
For each model, plot the cosine similarity cos(w_auth, w_syc) at every layer, not just the peak. This produces a “direction alignment trajectory” showing how authority and sycophancy become coupled through the network depth. The shape of this curve is informative: a sudden jump suggests a specific layer where authority gets “routed” into the sycophancy pathway; a gradual rise suggests distributed processing.
Subspace Decomposition
Project the sycophancy direction w_syc onto three orthogonal components: (1) the authority direction, (2) the confidence direction orthogonalized against authority, and (3) the residual. This decomposes sycophancy into “authority-driven,” “confidence-driven,” and “pure sycophancy” components. Report the fraction of variance explained by each.
Cross-Model Direction Similarity
For models sharing the Llama architecture (Llama-3.1-8B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B), directly compare their authority probe directions at matched layers using cosine similarity. If the same direction encodes authority in both models despite different alignment training, that’s evidence for a pre-training-induced authority feature that RLHF merely amplifies.

4. Remediation via Activation Steering
4.1 Method
The probed directions serve double duty: they are both the diagnostic tool (revealing the mechanism) and the therapeutic intervention (fixing it). For each model:
	•	Extract the authority probe direction w_auth at the layer with highest Probe A accuracy
	•	At inference time, subtract α × w_auth from the residual stream at that layer
	•	Re-run the full behavioral evaluation at α = {0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0}

4.2 Three Steering Comparisons
To rigorously attribute the effect, we steer with three different directions and compare:
	•	Steer with authority direction: Should specifically reduce persona-biased sycophancy while preserving general sycophancy resistance
	•	Steer with confidence direction: Tests whether removing confidence sensitivity is a better intervention
	•	Steer with sycophancy direction: The brute-force approach. Should reduce all sycophancy but may also reduce appropriate deference and helpfulness

6. Risk Analysis and Contingency Plans
6.1 Risk: The behavioral effect is too small
Scenario: High-authority personas produce only 2–3% more sycophancy than no-persona. The effect exists but is barely significant.
Contingency: Shift from raw flip rate to the softer Hedge Score and Deference Ratio metrics, which may show larger effects even when full answer-flipping is rare. Also test multi-turn escalation: does a single pushback not flip the model, but two or three pushbacks from a professor flip it while the same from a student doesn’t? Accumulated authority pressure may produce larger effects.
Silver lining: A null or weak behavioral result is still publishable if the probing analysis reveals that the model has the authority representation but doesn’t act on it. That’s a finding about robustness of current alignment — “RLHF-trained models can detect authority but resist deferring to it.”
6.2 Risk: The probing accuracy is too low
Scenario: Authority probe peaks at 60% accuracy — above chance but not enough to draw strong conclusions about directions.
Contingency: Switch from single-token probing to average-pooled probing across all tokens in the persona prefix. This gives the probe more signal. Alternatively, use nonlinear probes (2-layer MLP) to test whether authority is encoded nonlinearly. If a nonlinear probe succeeds where a linear one fails, that’s itself an interesting finding about representation structure.
6.3 Risk: TransformerLens doesn’t support a target model
Scenario: Gemma-2-9B or Qwen-2.5-7B fails to load in TransformerLens due to architectural differences.
Contingency: Use nnsight or baukit instead, which support arbitrary HuggingFace models via hooks. Alternatively, use raw PyTorch hooks (model.register_forward_hook) to capture activations — more code but fully general. Worst case: drop the problematic model and substitute another (e.g., Phi-3.5-mini-instruct or Yi-1.5-9B-Chat). Having 5 models gives us a buffer; the paper still works with 4.
6.4 Risk: The cosine similarity analysis is uninformative
Scenario: All cosine similarities are moderate (~0.3–0.5), making it hard to claim strong overlap or strong separation.
Contingency: Use the subspace decomposition analysis instead: project sycophancy onto authority + confidence + residual and report variance fractions. Also compute significance via permutation testing: shuffle the authority labels 1,000 times, recompute cosine similarity each time, and report the p-value for the observed overlap. This strengthens even a moderate finding.
6.5 Risk: Steering doesn’t reduce persona bias
Scenario: Subtracting the authority direction at any α either doesn’t change sycophancy or degrades it uniformly.
Contingency: Try steering at multiple layers simultaneously (the top 3 layers by probe accuracy). Also try the orthogonalized authority direction — the component of authority that’s orthogonal to confidence — to get a cleaner intervention. If nothing works, report the negative result honestly: “the authority direction is detectable but not straightforwardly steerable,” which suggests a more complex mechanistic pathway than simple linear encoding.
6.6 Risk: Running out of time
Priority ordering if behind schedule:
	•	Drop Day 8 (deep dive) entirely — it’s enrichment, not core
	•	Reduce from 5 models to 3 (keep Llama, Qwen, Mistral — the most architecturally diverse)
	•	Reduce steering sweep from 6 α values to 3 (0.5, 1.5, 3.0)
	•	Use only the authority steering direction, skip confidence and sycophancy comparison
	•	Last resort: submit as a 4-page short paper / extended abstract if full results aren’t ready

9. Novelty Claims and Anticipated Objections
9.1 Five Novelty Claims
	•	First measurement of persona-dependent sycophancy: No prior work has tested whether user identity claims affect model truthfulness. All sycophancy benchmarks use generic users.
	•	The matched vs. mismatched expertise test: Disentangling rational expertise weighting from blind authority deference. This specific experimental design is new.
	•	Mechanistic probing of authority processing in RLHF’d models: Nobody has probed for “authority detection” directions and measured their overlap with sycophancy directions.
	•	Cross-architecture mechanistic comparison: Five models from four labs, testing whether the authority–sycophancy linkage is universal or alignment-method-dependent.
	•	Direction-based remediation: Using the probed authority direction as a steering vector for targeted persona-bias reduction.
9.2 Anticipated Reviewer Objections
Objection: “The finding is obvious”
Response: The behavioral finding alone might be expected. But the paper’s contribution is mechanistic, not behavioral. The cosine similarity between authority and sycophancy directions — and whether it differs across alignment methods — is not predictable a priori. Furthermore, the matched vs. mismatched expertise result is genuinely uncertain: it could go either way, and the answer matters for whether this is a bug or a feature.
Objection: “Prompt-length confounds”
Response: We include length-matched neutral controls for every persona prefix. The control analysis (Day 3) explicitly validates that prompt length alone does not produce the effect.
Objection: “This is social psychology, not language modeling”
Response: The probing and steering analyses are squarely in the language modeling / mechanistic interpretability tradition. We use probing classifiers (Belinkov & Glass, 2019; Gurnee et al., 2023) and activation steering (Turner et al., 2023; Rimsky et al., 2024) — standard COLM-track tools — applied to a new question.
Objection: “50 questions is too few”
Response: With 50 questions × 16 conditions × 5 models = 4,000 conversations, we have strong statistical power for the factorial analysis. Each cell in the authority × model matrix has 50–200 observations. We report 95% CIs on all effect sizes.
Objection: “Users don’t really announce their credentials”
Response: They do in many real settings: patient-doctor interactions (“I’m also a physician”), customer support (“I’m a lawyer”), educational contexts, and enterprise deployments where user roles are injected into the prompt. We acknowledge this limitation and propose implicit authority signals (vocabulary complexity, formality) as future work.
