Factorial ANOVA (Analysis of Variance)
=======================================

What is ANOVA?
--------------
ANOVA tests whether there are statistically significant differences between
group means. For example: "Do models flip their answers more when the user
claims high authority vs low authority?"

A simple (one-way) ANOVA has one factor (e.g. authority level) with multiple
groups (none, low, medium, high). It answers: "Does the group you belong to
matter?"


What makes it "factorial"?
--------------------------
"Factorial" means you have MULTIPLE factors at the same time. Instead of
testing one variable in isolation, you test all of them together and also
test whether they interact.

In this project (see src/analysis/behavioral_stats.py), the three factors are:

  1. authority_level   (none, low, medium, high)
  2. domain_match      (matched, mismatched)
  3. confidence_level   (low, high)

A factorial ANOVA tells you:

  - MAIN EFFECTS: Does each factor independently affect sycophancy?
    e.g. "Does authority level matter, averaging over all confidence and
    domain match conditions?"

  - INTERACTION EFFECTS: Do factors combine in non-additive ways?
    e.g. "Is the authority effect stronger when confidence is high?"
    An interaction means the effect of one factor DEPENDS on the level
    of another factor.


How it works (simplified)
-------------------------
ANOVA decomposes the total variance in your data into parts:

  Total variance = Variance explained by Factor A
                 + Variance explained by Factor B
                 + Variance explained by A*B interaction
                 + Residual (unexplained) variance

Then for each effect it computes:

  F-statistic = (variance explained by effect) / (residual variance)

A large F means the effect explains much more variance than random noise.
The p-value tells you the probability of seeing an F this large by chance.


Key outputs
-----------
For each effect (main effects + interactions), the ANOVA table gives:

  - sum_sq     : Sum of squares (total variance explained by this effect)
  - df         : Degrees of freedom (related to number of groups)
  - F          : F-statistic (larger = stronger effect)
  - p_value    : Statistical significance (< 0.05 is conventional threshold)
  - eta_squared: Effect size = sum_sq(effect) / sum_sq(total)
                 Tells you what PROPORTION of variance this factor explains.
                 0.01 = small, 0.06 = medium, 0.14 = large


How this project uses it
------------------------
File: src/analysis/behavioral_stats.py, run_factorial_anova()

  formula = "flip ~ C(authority_level) * C(domain_match) * C(confidence_level)"

The "*" operator in the formula means "include all main effects AND all
interactions." So the model tests:

  Main effects:
    - C(authority_level)                         Does authority matter?
    - C(domain_match)                            Does domain match matter?
    - C(confidence_level)                        Does confidence matter?

  Two-way interactions:
    - C(authority_level):C(domain_match)         Does authority effect differ
                                                 by domain match?
    - C(authority_level):C(confidence_level)     Does authority effect differ
                                                 by confidence?
    - C(domain_match):C(confidence_level)        Does domain match effect differ
                                                 by confidence?

  Three-way interaction:
    - C(authority_level):C(domain_match):C(confidence_level)
                                                 Do all three interact?

The dependent variable (dv) is "flip" -- a binary variable (0 or 1) indicating
whether the model changed its correct answer to the wrong one.

Control conditions (is_control=True) are EXCLUDED before running the ANOVA,
because controls don't fit into the factorial design (they have special
authority levels like "none" and "control_high").

Type III sum of squares (typ=3) is used, which tests each effect after
accounting for all other effects. This is the standard choice when group
sizes are unequal or when you care about interactions.


Why not just compare percentages?
---------------------------------
You could just look at flip rates per group (and we do, for quick checks).
But factorial ANOVA adds:

  1. Statistical rigor: p-values tell you if differences are real or noise
  2. Interaction detection: You can't see interactions by looking at one
     factor at a time
  3. Effect sizes: eta-squared quantifies HOW MUCH each factor matters
  4. Controls for confounds: Testing factors simultaneously avoids
     Simpson's paradox (where a trend in subgroups reverses in aggregate)


Other stats used alongside ANOVA in this project
-------------------------------------------------
  - Logistic regression (run_logistic_regression): Similar question but models
    the binary flip outcome directly. Gives odds ratios -- e.g. "high authority
    makes a flip 3.2x more likely than low authority."

  - Cohen's d (compute_effect_size): Standardized difference between two group
    means. Used for pairwise comparisons like high-vs-none authority.
    0.2 = small, 0.5 = medium, 0.8 = large.

  - Two-proportion z-test (matched_vs_mismatched_analysis): Tests the specific
    hypothesis about blind authority deference vs rational expertise weighting.
